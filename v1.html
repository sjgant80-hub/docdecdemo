How it works — traced from a real run

The grounding is a plain, auditable function over your documents' distinctive terms — you can read exactly why it answered or refused, and the citation points at the paragraph that decided it. It also applies a relevance check: it abstains when the best paragraph isn't actually relevant to the question (not just sharing words), using a local-embedding signal — on your hardware, nothing sent out. Structure still picks and cites the answer; the check only vetoes a weak match. (The production engine deepens the routing privately; the behaviours — grounds, cites, abstains — are the same.)

Why it matters — for each role

For the person asking

An answer you can trust because it shows its source — and a straight "that's not in your documents" instead of a confident invention. The failure mode of RAG (a fluent wrong answer) is the one that costs you.

For Compliance / Risk

Every answer is auditable: it cites the exact paragraph, or it abstains. Grounded-or-silent is the property a regulator, a lawyer, or an auditor actually asks for — and the one a nearest-chunk retriever can't give.

For IT / Security

Sovereign. The documents and the index stay on your hardware; nothing is sent to a vendor cloud per query. The grounding runs on CPU — no per-question API bill.

For the data owner

Point it at your corpus; it answers from that, cites it, and refuses what isn't there. Replace a disappointing RAG at the retrieval seam without re-platforming.

Adoption — how you'd roll it out

1 · Try your corpus

Point DocDec at a slice of your own documents and run your real questions — including the ones you know aren't covered, to watch it abstain.

2 · Sit it beside your RAG

Run the same questions through your current retriever and through DocDec; compare hallucinations, citations, and what each refuses. The before/after is the decision.

3 · Replace the seam

Swap DocDec in at the retrieval/answer boundary. It stays on your hardware; the answers come back grounded and cited.

Honest limits — the part a sales page usually hides

Grounding is a trade, and this benchmark shows it honestly — the numbers above are measured, not chosen. On SQuAD 2.0's answerable questions DocDec's recall sits below the vector-RAG's: a router that refuses weak matches answers fewer questions than one that always guesses. In exchange it answers with markedly higher precision, cites every answer, and — the point — abstains on a large share of the questions the documents don't cover, where the RAG hallucinates on every single one. (An earlier version of this page abstained on far fewer; a local-embedding relevance check more than tripled that, and the numbers above are the improved result.)

And it is not a magic no-hallucination box. SQuAD 2.0's "unanswerable" questions are adversarial — written to look answerable from a specific paragraph — so a transparent term-grounding router is fooled on many of them (the exact rate is in the table above, not spun). On questions about other subjects — the common real case — it is a different story, and now a measured one: asked — real questions from other Wikipedia articles, the full DocDec refused —. (Real questions written by people about other topics; a different kind of off-topic question could do worse — we'd measure on yours.) The in-browser free box, which has only the structural part, refuses — of the same questions; before 16 November it was tuned like the benchmark and refused only — — we found that, fixed it, and kept the before-number here. The honest summary: DocDec moves you from "always answers, often wrong" to "answers with a citation, or admits it doesn't know," and the measured abstention strengthens as the routing deepens in the production engine. Those numbers are a public-corpus demo, not a promise about your documents — we'd measure on yours.
